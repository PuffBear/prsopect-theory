\begin{abstract}
Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) can collapse into a degenerate
\emph{lazy-agent} policy when reward centralization is too low, but the field describes this
failure only qualitatively. Our contribution is a way to measure it: a pre-registered collapse
definition and bootstrap confidence intervals that turn the failure into a critical
centralization $\wcrit$ with error bars---to our knowledge the first such measurement in deep
cooperative MARL. We then use the instrument to ask whether behavioral preferences move the
threshold, and find that they do: \emph{loss aversion} systematically shifts $\wcrit$ rightward, so
loss-averse agents need more centralization to coordinate. This effect is monotone in the
loss-aversion strength $\lambda$ and replicates in direction across three environments and two
algorithms; at the human range ($\lambda\!\approx\!3$) it adds $+0.089$ to the required
centralization. We report what is robust (the boundary, and the direction of the loss-aversion
effect) separately from what is substrate-specific (the effect's magnitude and functional form),
and offer a supporting mean-field account at the level of sign, not magnitude. The pre-registered
protocol is a reusable instrument for measuring where any cooperative system collapses, and how far
behavioral bias moves that point.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A team of learning agents sharing a task can fall into a trap their designers never
intended: instead of coordinating, every agent quietly defaults to inaction, and the team
settles into a stable, useless equilibrium. Cooperative MARL calls this \emph{coordination
collapse}, with the \emph{lazy-agent} policy as its endpoint~\citep{liu2023lazy,peng2023curo}---an
instance of the sequential social dilemma that arises whenever private incentives drift from
the collective good~\citep{leibo2017multi}. Whether a team falls in is governed by one design
dial, the \emph{reward centralization} $w\in[0,1]$: the share of each agent's reward tied to
the team's outcome rather than its own. Turn $w$ down and an agent's private performance
drowns out its contribution, so cooperating earns no learnable signal and the safe policy
wins. The field knows this only as a direction---\emph{more centralization
helps}~\citep{dmarl2025,liu2023lazy}---and stops there. \emph{Where}, in $w$, a working team
tips into collapse has never been measured. Our central question is therefore twofold: does
coordination collapse admit a \emph{measurable} critical centralization threshold, and do
behavioral preferences systematically shift it? The first half is a measurement problem---the
paper's primary contribution---and the second is what the measurement lets us ask. The nearest work mitigates the lazy-agent
pathology without locating a boundary~\citep{liu2023lazy,peng2023curo,weng2026}, or sweeps
neighbouring axes---credit-assignment level~\citep{wang2025maca}, reward
weights~\citep{jiang2018dgn}. Closest in spirit, \citet{yamaguchi2025} build a phase diagram
for independent Q-learning over \emph{environmental} axes (grid size, density), and
\citet{capannoli2026phase} prove a metastable boundary in network opinion dynamics under a
fixed update rule; neither measures a reward-structure threshold in gradient-trained
cooperative policies, with confidence intervals, that a behavioral bias then moves.

Pinning that number down matters now, because the agents being deployed are no longer neutral
optimizers. In automated replenishment, fraud review, and energy dispatch, learning agents
act for or beside people and absorb human preference structure by construction---whether
trained on human choices through preference-aligned objectives that are themselves
prospect-theoretic in form~\citep{ethayarajh2024kto}, or sharing the loop with human
operators~\citep{lalmohammed2025}. The sturdiest such regularity is \emph{loss aversion}: a
loss looms larger than an equal gain~\citep{kahneman1979,tversky1992}. Behavioral economics
knows what this does to one chooser; it is silent on what it does to a \emph{group} learning
to coordinate~\citep{leibo2017multi,lowe2017multi}. A measured collapse boundary is the
instrument that question needs, and it extends a thin existing bridge between behavioral
preferences and collective learning dynamics---inequity aversion in social
dilemmas~\citep{hughes2018inequity}, deterministic limits of multi-agent
learning~\citep{barfuss2019deterministic,hu2019meanfield}---to the prospect-theoretic
preferences that today's training pipelines instill.

We therefore build the ruler first, and treat it as the paper's central object. Following the
posture physics takes toward a phase boundary, we fix a binary collapse criterion in advance,
freeze it, fit $P(\mathrm{collapse}\mid w)$, and bootstrap confidence intervals on $\wcrit$.
(We mean ``phase boundary'' empirically---a sharp, metastable threshold whose
$T\!\to\!\infty$ limit is the boundary proper---not a thermodynamic singularity.) Two design
choices keep the readings honest: we apply cumulative prospect theory (CPT) as a \emph{reward
transform} after blending rather than inside the update, so the optimizer is bit-identical
across conditions and any movement of $\wcrit$ is behavioral; and we re-measure at several
horizons, since a drifting threshold could pass for a constant. Our \emph{primary} contribution is
(i) the first confidence-intervalled measurement of the coordination-collapse boundary in deep
cooperative MARL, with a reusable pre-registered protocol. Using that instrument, we then establish
three findings about behavioral bias: (ii) loss aversion shifts the boundary rightward, a direction
whose sign we fix a priori and confirm across three environments and two algorithms, with the
metastable horizon slide generalizing likewise; (iii) prospect theory's two components move it in
\emph{opposite} directions; and (iv) on a directed-flow substrate, a downstream loss-averse
\emph{minority} can set the boundary for the whole system. Our novelty is narrow: \citet{mengesha2025} show loss aversion moves
a tipping point, but in evolutionary game theory over fixed strategies, and
\citet{lalmohammed2025} bring CPT into cooperative MARL without separating its components or
measuring a boundary. Ours is the measured, CI'd boundary and the component decomposition
against it, in gradient-trained neural policies.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Coordination collapse and credit assignment.}
Cooperative MARL has been studied since independent $Q$-learning on shared
tasks~\citep{tan1993multi,busoniu2008comprehensive}, and deep variants inherit its
pathologies---the lazy-agent and relative-overgeneralization failures in which agents abandon
cooperation for a degenerate policy~\citep{liu2023lazy,peng2023curo,tampuu2017multiagent}. The
standard remedy is centralized credit assignment under CTDE: a centralized critic or a
factorized value function that decomposes team reward into per-agent
contributions~\citep{lowe2017multi,yu2022surprising,sunehag2018vdn,rashid2018qmix,foerster2018counterfactual,son2019qtran},
benchmarked on suites such as SMAC~\citep{samvelyan2019smac}. \citet{weng2026} attribute the
failure to representation degradation under decentralized exploration, and \citet{dmarl2025}
show decentralized shaping cannot match centralized baselines. This literature establishes
\emph{that} centralization helps and \emph{how} to implement it; none locates the threshold
$\wcrit$ at which it becomes necessary, or attaches a confidence interval to it---the gap we
close, using the centralization weight $w$ as the dial rather than a fixed factorization.

\paragraph{Phase transitions in multi-agent learning.}
Treating collective learning as a dynamical system with critical points has a growing
precedent. \citet{yamaguchi2025} construct a phase diagram for independent Q-learning over
\emph{environmental} axes (grid size, agent density), and \citet{capannoli2026phase} prove a
metastable partial-adoption boundary in network opinion dynamics under a fixed update rule.
Closest in method, the deterministic-limit and mean-field programmes for multi-agent
learning~\citep{barfuss2019deterministic,hu2019meanfield,hussain2025convergence} derive
Fokker--Planck descriptions of networked Q-learning and tie convergence to exploration and
sparsity. We share their statistical-physics posture but differ in object: ours is a
\emph{reward-structure} threshold in gradient-trained cooperative policies, with confidence
intervals, that a \emph{behavioral} parameter then moves---and we apply the mean-field
machinery as a known tool to fix the sign, not as the contribution itself.

\paragraph{Behavioral preferences in cooperative AI.}
A thin bridge connects human preference structure to collective learning dynamics:
\citet{hughes2018inequity} show inequity aversion improves cooperation in social dilemmas,
and the cooperative-AI agenda~\citep{dafoe2020open,hammond2025multiagent,barfuss2020caring}
frames behavioral and safety considerations for multi-agent systems. On the risk-sensitive
side, CVaR objectives optimize tail outcomes~\citep{qiu2021rmix,xie2023riskq,prashanth2014cvar,tamar2015optimizing}---distinct
from loss aversion, which is a reference-point asymmetry rather than a tail reweighting. Prospect
theory has been brought into single-agent RL for prediction and
control~\citep{prashanth2016cumulative}, and its calibration concerns are well
known~\citep{rabin2000risk,tversky1992advances}. In the multi-agent setting,
\citet{mengesha2025} prove loss aversion shifts a coordination tipping point, but in evolutionary
game theory over fixed strategies; \citet{lalmohammed2025} bring CPT into cooperative MARL but
fold it into the actor--critic update, entangling its components. Our work extends this bridge to
prospect-theoretic preferences in gradient-trained \emph{cooperative} policies and, uniquely in
this line, \emph{decomposes} prospect theory's components against a measured boundary.

\section{Preliminaries and Method}
\label{sec:method}

In cooperative MARL, $N$ agents share an environment and maximize a joint objective under
CTDE~\citep{lowe2017multi}; the reward-mixing weight $w\in[0,1]$ interpolates purely local
($w{=}0$) and purely global ($w{=}1$) reward. As $w\!\to\!0$ the cooperative signal is diluted
and agents collapse to the safe lazy policy~\citep{liu2023lazy,peng2023curo}. This $w$ is our
sole structural intervention; all else is held fixed, so any boundary shift is attributable to
$w$ and the behavioral parameters. Cumulative prospect theory~\citep{kahneman1979,tversky1992}
gives the value function $v(x)=x^\alpha$ for $x\ge0$ and $-\lambda(-x)^\beta$ for $x<0$, where
$\alpha,\beta\in(0,1]$ govern curvature and $\lambda>1$ is loss aversion
($\alpha{=}\beta{=}1,\lambda{=}1$ is linear utility). We apply it as a reward transform after
blending,
\begin{equation}
  r_i^{\PT}=v\!\left((1-w)\,r_i^{\mathrm{local}}+w\,r^{\mathrm{global}}\right),
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
the decisive difference from \citet{lalmohammed2025}, who fold CPT \emph{into} the
actor--critic update, entangling curvature with loss aversion and confounding any shift with a
change to the optimizer. Transforming only the scalar reward leaves the policy gradient
identical across conditions, so a measured shift is behavioral and the two channels dial
independently. We pre-registered the binary collapse label
$\mathbf{1}[\bar S<10\wedge\bar\pi\le-127.1]$ ($\bar S$ mean base-stock, $\bar\pi$ mean profit),
freezing it in version control before any analysis (the commit hash is withheld for anonymity and
released on acceptance); it is robust to $\pm20\%$ threshold perturbation (max $\Delta\wcrit=0.015$;
Supplement). For
each condition we fit $P(\mathrm{collapse}\mid w)=\sigma(b_0+b_1 w)$, estimate
$\wcrit=-b_0/b_1$ (the $w$ at which $P(\mathrm{collapse})=0.5$), and bootstrap its 95\% CI; steep
high-$\lambda$ cells use Firth penalized regression, which agrees with the bootstrap throughout.
The logistic midpoint is the natural transition point for two reasons. First, collapse is a
\emph{binary} outcome per run, so the sigmoid is the maximum-entropy model of its $w$-dependence
and $-b_0/b_1$ is the location parameter---the $w$ of maximal uncertainty, where a run is equally
likely to coordinate or collapse. Second, the transition is empirically \emph{sharp}: collapse
fractions rise from near-zero to near-one within $\Delta w<0.3$ (below), so the midpoint is not an
arbitrary cut through a gentle slope but the center of a narrow, well-identified crossing whose
width we report ($\Delta w$) alongside its location. We therefore treat $\wcrit$ as a measured
threshold with a confidence interval, and are explicit that ``phase boundary'' is used in this
empirical sense, not as a claim of thermodynamic criticality. The decomposition compares three
conditions with identical environment and optimizer: \textbf{Null} ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}1,
\lambda{=}1$), \textbf{Curvature} ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}0.88,\lambda{=}1$), and \textbf{Loss
aversion} ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}0.88,\lambda>1$). The primary substrate is
\texttt{NetworkManagement-v1}~\citep{hubbs2020orgym}, a six-node multi-echelon inventory network
that extends prior deep RL inventory work~\citep{oroojlooyjadid2022,gijsbrechts2022,marlim2023};
the transfer environments are MPE Cooperative Navigation~\citep{lowe2017multi} and Level-Based
Foraging~\citep{christianos2020shared}. Agents run PPO~\citep{schulman2017ppo} via
Stable-Baselines3~\citep{raffin2021sb3} and PettingZoo/SuperSuit~\citep{terry2021pettingzoo,terry2020supersuit}
(20 seeds/condition, 1{,}524 primary runs). Full setup and hyperparameters are in the Supplement.

\section{The Boundary and Its Behavioral Shift}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{A sharp, metastable boundary.}
Collapse is a sharp logistic transition in $w$, not a gradual decay: at 300k steps
$\wcrit=0.666$ $[0.635,0.694]$ (transition width $\Delta w=0.199$, $p=3.2\times10^{-7}$), with
collapse fractions rising from near-zero to near-one within $\Delta w<0.3$
(Figure~\ref{fig:boundary}a). The boundary is not a constant but a \emph{metastable} threshold that
slides left with the training horizon, and across four matched risk-neutral horizons
($\wcrit=0.822,0.666,0.622,0.573$ at $150\mathrm{k},300\mathrm{k},450\mathrm{k},900\mathrm{k}$) the
slide is \emph{log-linear in $T$}: $\wcrit=2.44-0.138\ln T$ (RMSE $0.027$; all four CIs of width
${\le}0.04$; Figure~\ref{fig:boundary}b). This log-linear form is \emph{consistent with} metastable
escape (a Kramers-type first-passage picture; \S\ref{sec:mechanism}): each doubling of the budget
buys a fixed $\approx0.10$ leftward shift in $w$, so the boundary keeps falling below the longest
measured point
($0.573$ at 900k)---extrapolating the law to $2\mathrm{M}$ steps gives $\wcrit\approx0.44$. Collapse
at intermediate $w$ is therefore the metastable \emph{approach} to the deterministic floor
$\wcrit^0=c/(c+g)$ the mechanism derives (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}), escapable with more training
rather than absorbing; we report the slide and its rate, and treat the floor's exact value as below
the measured range rather than extrapolating the log-linear form to an (unphysical) zero crossing. The slide is not a supply-chain artifact either: under centralized-critic MAPPO it recurs
in both transfer environments---on MPE $\wcrit$ falls $0.82\to0.68$ as the horizon grows
$2\mathrm{M}\to8\mathrm{M}$, and on Level-Based Foraging
$0.33\to0.20$ over $1\mathrm{M}\to4\mathrm{M}$, both with non-overlapping intervals. A
basin-control experiment rules out initialization as the driver (Supplement).

\paragraph{Loss aversion raises the boundary; curvature lowers it.}
Against a single canonical null---$\wcrit^{\mathrm{null}}=0.666$ $[0.635,0.694]$, independently
reproduced at $0.685$ by a dedicated experiment (every other null-like number is a labeled
offset, not a separate baseline)---we sweep $\lambda\in\{1,\dots,7\}$ ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}0.88$,
300k). The boundary rises monotonically with loss aversion---$\{0.586,0.715,0.774,0.806,0.839,
0.836,0.851\}$---then saturates near $0.85$ with clear headroom to the $w{=}1$ ceiling, so the
plateau is behavioral, not a ceiling artifact (Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition},
Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}); the endpoints are non-overlapping by $0.221$. Curvature acts in
the \emph{opposite} direction, \emph{lowering} $\wcrit$ to $0.586$ ($-0.100$ $[-0.172,-0.033]$
vs.\ the null). That the two components of a single ``risk-averse'' psychology pull the boundary
in opposite directions is the first of two surprises, and is visible only because there is a
calibrated null to read each component against.

\paragraph{Two ways to count the loss-aversion effect.}
The magnitude depends on the baseline, and we report both explicitly to avoid ambiguity
(Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}). Measured \emph{against the linear null} ($0.685$), the
combined CPT shift is $+0.089$ $[0.041,0.141]$ at $\lambda{=}3$ and $+0.166$ $[0.135,0.202]$ at
$\lambda{=}7$---but this nets loss aversion against curvature's $-0.100$. The \emph{pure
loss-aversion increment}, measured against the curvature baseline ($\lambda{=}1$, $0.586$)---the
correct isolate, since it holds curvature fixed---is larger: $+0.188$ at $\lambda{=}3$ and
$+0.265$ at $\lambda{=}7$. The two are reconciled by the curvature offset ($-0.100+0.265=0.165
\approx0.166$). Throughout, ``the loss-aversion effect'' refers to the increment over the
curvature baseline; the net-vs-null figure is the deployment-relevant total. The full sweep is
tracked by a one-parameter, theory-bounded affine law (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}) to RMSE $0.014$. A
finer $w\in\{0.80,0.90\}$ grid resolves the steep high-$\lambda$ cells: the $\lambda{\geq}5$
boundary is tight and cleanly monotone ($0.825,0.835,0.852$ at $\lambda{=}5,6,7$, CI widths
$0.038,0.033,0.024$---about half those from a single $w{=}0.85$ cell).

\section{A Mean-Field Mechanism Fixes the Sign}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The measurement above is the paper's contribution; this section adds a \emph{supporting} account of
why the boundary exists and which way each component moves it. It is deliberately modest---sign and
functional form, not magnitude, and not the basis of any empirical claim---so the results stand
whether or not the reader accepts the mechanism. Following deterministic-limit and mean-field
treatments of multi-agent
learning~\citep{barfuss2019deterministic,hu2019meanfield,hussain2025convergence}, we model each
Gaussian-policy PPO agent's mean action as a Langevin variable; the machinery is standard and is
in the Supplement. Modelling the policy mean as $\mathrm{d}\mu_i=D(w)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\Theta(w)}\,
\mathrm{d}W$, with drift $D$ and a centralization-weighted diffusion $\Theta$ into which partner
exploration enters, the replenishing regime makes an upstream node's inventory a local cost but
a global benefit, giving cooperative drift $D(w)=\eta[wg-(1-w)c]$. Escape from the lazy basin is
governed not by drift but by its ratio to gradient noise,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SNR}(w)=\frac{D(w)}{\sqrt{\Theta(w)}}\gtrsim\kappa_c,
  \label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}
and the crossing defines $\wcrit$. Treating escape as a Kramers first-passage
problem~\citep{kramers1940brownian} ($\tau\sim e^{\Delta U/\Theta}$)---the noise-induced analogue of
a saddle-node bifurcation of the coordinating basin~\citep{strogatz1994nonlinear}---gives two
qualitative expectations \emph{consistent with} the data: collapse switches abruptly across $\wcrit$
(the sharp fits) and $\wcrit(T)$ falls as $T$ grows (the metastable slide). We do \emph{not} derive
the numerical value of $\wcrit$: $c,g,\sigma,\kappa_c$ are calibrated, and our pre-registered direct
drift probe was \emph{inconclusive}---it reproduces the matched-horizon boundary in collapse
fraction but does not localize $\wcrit$, because survivor bias in the contested band confounds the
conditioned drift (Supplement; a non-survivor-biased re-probe is the natural fix). The magnitude
therefore remains calibrated; the mechanism's content is the sign and the form.

We are direct about the one place the account could be read as circular. The sign result below
assumes a payoff premise ($\partial_\lambda F<0$: loss aversion inflates loss-domain gradient
variance, lowering the cooperative signal-to-noise ratio). We do \emph{not} take this on faith or
infer it from the outcome---we verify it independently with a finite-difference probe of the
substrate (Supplement), so the proposition's content is the non-trivial claim that this
\emph{locally} measured sign \emph{propagates} to the \emph{global} boundary through the intractable
noise, not the assumption that loss aversion hurts coordination.

\begin{proposition}[Sign propagation of the loss-aversion shift]\label{thm:sign}
Let the order parameter $x$ evolve as $\mathrm{d}x_t=F(x_t,w,\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}t+
\sqrt{\Sigma(x_t,N)}\,\mathrm{d}W_t$ with $\Sigma>0$, and \emph{assume the payoff premise}
$\partial_\lambda F<0$ on the escape interval---i.e.\ loss aversion scales only the loss domain
and so inflates gradient noise. Then $\partial_\lambda \wcrit^{\mathrm{Emp}}>0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The Kramers barrier $\Delta U=\int 2F/\Sigma\,\mathrm{d}z$ has $\partial_\lambda\Delta U<0$
pointwise, since $\Sigma>0$ and $\partial_\lambda F<0$ throughout, independent of the sign of
$F$; a lower barrier shortens escape ($\partial_\lambda\tau<0$) while $\partial_w F>0$ lengthens
it. The implicit function theorem on $\tau(w,\lambda)=T$ gives $\mathrm{d}\wcrit^{\mathrm{Emp}}/
\mathrm{d}\lambda=-\partial_\lambda\tau/\partial_w\tau>0$. The magnitude depends on the
intractable $\Sigma$; the direction does not. The same argument with curvature's drift signs
reversed yields the opposite shift.
\end{proof}

We state this as a \emph{proposition}, not a theorem, because its force is conditional: it assumes
the payoff premise $\partial_\lambda F<0$ rather than deriving it from first principles. We verify
that premise directly on the substrate (a finite-difference probe confirms loss aversion inflates
the loss-domain gradient variance; Supplement), so the result's content is that this \emph{local}
sign propagates to the \emph{global} boundary regardless of the intractable noise $\Sigma$---which
is exactly what makes the direction (but not the magnitude) predictable. The same drift analysis fixes the opposite sign
of the two prospect-theory components without fitting: curvature ($\alpha,\beta<1$) compresses
both gains and losses, lowering the variance in $\Theta$, raising the SNR, and so \emph{lowering}
$\wcrit$, while loss aversion inflates $\Theta$ and \emph{raises} it---matching the measured
$-0.100$ and $+0.265$ (Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}).

\paragraph{A theory-bounded law, tested out of sample.}
The same loss-domain split fixes the functional \emph{form}, not just the sign. Only the loss
domain scales with $\lambda$, giving
\begin{equation}
  \wcrit(\lambda)=\frac{\phi(\lambda)}{\phi(\lambda)+g/c},\qquad \phi(\lambda)=1+\rho(\lambda-1),
  \label{eq:wcrit_lambda}
\end{equation}
with $\rho\in[0,1]$ \emph{bounded by the theory}---a genuine parametric constraint, not a free
curve fit. We fix $g/c$ from the $\lambda{=}1$ baseline and $\rho=0.61$ from three calibration
points ($\lambda\in\{1,3,7\}$), then test \emph{out of sample} against the held-out four
($\lambda\in\{2,4,5,6\}$): $\wcrit^{\mathrm{pred}}=\{0.586,0.695,0.759,0.800,0.830,0.851,0.868\}$
versus $\wcrit^{\mathrm{meas}}=\{0.586,0.715,0.774,0.806,0.839,0.836,0.851\}$, RMSE $0.014$
across all seven points (Figure~\ref{fig:affine}). The one systematic residual is a mild concave
overshoot at the plateau, consistent with agents endogenously reducing loss exposure as
$\lambda$ grows. We report this derived, bounded law rather than a better-fitting power law
because the point is mechanism, not goodness of fit---and we are explicit that it is
\emph{single-substrate}: it does not transfer to LBF (\S\ref{sec:generalization}). Table~\ref{tab:ledger}
states what the mechanism derives a priori, what is empirically confirmed, and what stays
calibrated.

\section{The Direction Generalizes; the Magnitudes Do Not}
\label{sec:generalization}

The mechanism commits in advance to a direction: loss aversion inflates gradient noise and so
raises $\wcrit$. If that is right rather than an artifact of one substrate or optimizer, the sign
should survive changing both. We test on two further environments and a second algorithm,
matching the learner to each environment's learnability (independent PPO where coordination is
locally learnable; centralized-critic MAPPO~\citep{yu2022surprising} where the reward is
shared-global). The direction is robust in every setting; the absolute level, the curvature sign,
and the functional form are not (Figure~\ref{fig:generalization}, Table~\ref{tab:generalization}).
On the \emph{supply chain under MAPPO} (only the learner changes), $\wcrit$ rises $0.796\to0.889$
over $\lambda{=}1\to7$, monotone from $\lambda{=}2$, endpoints non-overlapping; the level differs
at low $\lambda$ but converges with PPO by $\lambda\!\geq\!4$. On \emph{MPE} the loss-aversion
excess is $+0.158$ $[0.108,0.212]$ at $\lambda{=}7$, but the curvature effect is null/opposite
($+0.042$, CI spanning zero), and on \emph{LBF} curvature moves the boundary \emph{right}---both
confirming the curvature sign is substrate-specific. The supply chain's affine law misfits LBF by
RMSE $0.15$. Thus the \emph{method}---a CI'd boundary that loss aversion shifts---generalizes
across three environments and two algorithms, while the affine constants and the curvature sign do
not.

\paragraph{Two boundary conditions, both predicted.}
The mechanism makes the boundary contingent on two ingredients, and ablating each confirms it
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). \emph{Action representation.} The signal-to-noise crossing is a
property of a continuous gradient, so destroying it should remove the boundary. Discretizing the
base-stock control into $K{=}11$ bins and running value-based learners (QMIX, IQL) yields a flat,
$w$-independent collapse rate with no fittable $\wcrit$. Crucially, a single-variable control
isolates the cause: PPO on the \emph{same} discrete shell also goes flat, so it is coarse
discretization---not the value-based paradigm---that erases the boundary, exactly as the
continuous-gradient account predicts. (This is not a claim that discreteness per se removes the
boundary: MPE is natively discrete and retains it.) \emph{Credit assignment.} On a shared-reward
task the boundary is measurable only with a learner that can coordinate at all: independent PPO
collapses at \emph{every} $w$ on MPE---a coordination floor masking the behavioral boundary---while
centralized-critic MAPPO recovers it. Neither is a negative result; each is a confirmed scope
condition of the phenomenon.

\section{A Minority Sets the Boundary}
\label{sec:minority}

The decomposition treats the population as homogeneous, but deployed systems mix loss-averse and
risk-neutral participants. Holding the substrate, PPO config, label, and grid identical to the main
sweep, we ask two questions: how much bias at a critical node is needed to move the boundary, and
which node's bias matters most (Figure~\ref{fig:cascade}).

\paragraph{A single node, dose for dose.}
Sweeping the most downstream trainable node's loss aversion $\lambda\in\{1,\dots,7\}$ with all
others risk-neutral traces a clean, strictly monotone dose-response: $\wcrit$ rises
$0.586\to0.856$ and converges on the \emph{fully} loss-averse population value ($0.851$) by
$\lambda{=}7$ (Figure~\ref{fig:cascade}b). One biased node, biased hard enough, imposes the
centralization requirement of the entire network---the homogeneous result is the saturation of a
continuous law, not a special case.

\paragraph{Position: proximity to demand, not chain index.}
Placing a single $\lambda{=}7$ node at each position, the effect is strongly position-dependent,
but the governing variable is how directly a node's output reaches demand, not a one-dimensional
chain index (Figure~\ref{fig:cascade}a). The two nodes feeding retail directly dominate ($0.856$
and $0.800$, the strongest at the all-loss-averse boundary); among upstream suppliers, the one
feeding \emph{only} the dominant downstream node is nearly as strong ($0.797$), while those
\emph{splitting} output across both downstream nodes are weaker ($0.726$, $0.673$). The network is
a branching DAG, not a series chain, and $\wcrit$ tracks \emph{path concentration}: a biased node
matters more the more directly and exclusively its product flows to demand. That a behavioral
minority moves a tipping point is known in evolutionary game theory~\citep{mengesha2025}; ours is
the deep-MARL instantiation, with a topological signature absent from that account---audit the
participant whose output most directly reaches demand, not the average or merely ``upstream'' one.
The result is single-substrate \emph{by construction}: ``which node sets the boundary'' is
well-posed only under a directed resource flow, which MPE and LBF lack (Supplement). We frame its
reach as a \emph{falsifiable} prediction: in any directed-flow cooperative domain, the boundary is
set by the biased participant of highest path concentration to demand.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

That probability weighting and loss aversion exert opposite effects on the boundary is, to our
knowledge, novel; prior work applies prospect theory as a unified model without decomposing
it~\citep{lalmohammed2025,ghaemi2024risk}. The operational reading is concrete: a system of
$\lambda{=}7$ agents needs $w>0.851$ for reliable coordination against $w>0.685$ for risk-neutral
agents---a $+0.166$ deployment margin that scales with $\lambda$, so domains nearer
$\lambda\!\approx\!2$--$3$ incur a smaller but positive cost. Where centralization is capped by
privacy or communication cost, the boundary reframes the problem, exposing $\lambda$---the agents'
effective loss aversion, shaped by the training objective---as a second lever. Our phase-diagram
framing sits within a recognized line of multiagent phase-transition
results~\citep{yamaguchi2025,capannoli2026phase} and the broader project of cooperative
AI~\citep{dafoe2020open,hammond2025multiagent,barfuss2020caring}; its distinctive contribution is
a boundary set by the policy-gradient signal-to-noise ratio rather than a fixed update rule.

\paragraph{What a practitioner does with this.}
The boundary is a design quantity. A team deploying cooperative agents can \emph{estimate} its own
$\wcrit$ with the pre-registered protocol (turning ``how much should we centralize?'' into a
measured target with error bars), \emph{budget} for the loss-aversion margin on top of the
risk-neutral requirement rather than discovering collapse in production, and---where $w$ is capped
by privacy or communication cost---treat $\lambda$ as the second lever the decomposition exposes,
lowering agents' \emph{effective} loss aversion through the training objective instead of raising
$w$. The minority result adds a targeting rule: audit the biased participant whose output most
directly reaches demand, not the average agent. The relevant $\lambda$ is itself set by how an agent
was trained---preference-aligned objectives are prospect-theoretic in
form~\citep{ethayarajh2024kto,tversky1992advances}---so estimating it from behavior, against the
calibration debates around prospect theory~\citep{rabin2000risk}, ties the behavioral axis to a
measurable property of the training pipeline. Laboratory loss aversion is
$\lambda\!\approx\!2.25$, so the deployment-relevant margins sit near our $\lambda{=}3$ figure.

\paragraph{Scope and Future Work.}
Three boundaries of the present results each point to a concrete next step. \emph{(i) From one
substrate to many.} The \emph{quantitative} core---the $\wcrit(\lambda)$ law and the cascade---is
established on a single directed-flow substrate by construction; we replicate the boundary, the
loss-aversion direction, and the metastable slide across environments and a second algorithm, but
the precise magnitudes are a property of one simulator and one compute budget, and the law itself
does not transfer to LBF. Replicating the cascade on a second directed-flow domain (e.g.\ congested
traffic routing or staged resource allocation) would convert ``single-substrate by construction''
into a tested generalization. \emph{(ii) From calibrated to predictive.} The mechanism fixes sign
and form but not magnitude: our one direct probe of the policy-gradient drift was
\emph{inconclusive}, reproducing the boundary in collapse fraction but failing to localize $\wcrit$
because the policy initializes above the cooperative optimum, so collapses cross the contested band
too fast to sample and bias the conditioned drift toward survivors. A probe that initializes near
the cooperative optimum, or measures a basin-flow statistic rather than the conditioned drift, would
turn the magnitude from a fit into a prediction and discharge the payoff premise that
Proposition~\ref{thm:sign} currently assumes---the single change that would most strengthen the
account. \emph{(iii) From bracket to calibration.} We sweep $\lambda$ to a high-stakes upper bound
of $7$, beyond the typical human value ($\lambda\!\approx\!2.25$, with deployment-relevant margins
near $\lambda\!\approx\!3$); estimating the effective $\lambda$ of preference-aligned agents from
behavior would tie the behavioral axis to a measurable property of the training pipeline. None of
these undercut the central, replicated claim---loss aversion raises the coordination
requirement---but each marks where measurement currently ends and prediction begins.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented the first confidence-intervalled phase diagram of cooperative-MARL coordination
collapse, characterizing $\wcrit$ as a function of reward structure, training horizon, and
prospect-theoretic loss aversion. Loss aversion impedes coordination---raising the requirement, a
direction we predict a priori and confirm across four environment--algorithm settings, with the
metastable slide generalizing likewise. Two further results sharpen the picture: prospect theory's
two components act in opposite directions, and a downstream loss-averse minority can set the
boundary on a directed-flow substrate, framed as a falsifiable prediction. Beyond the findings, the
pre-registered phase-diagram protocol---a frozen collapse label, a logistic fit over a small
$w$-sweep, and a bootstrap interval on $\wcrit$---is a reusable instrument: any cooperative-MARL
study can report \emph{where} its system collapses, with error bars, rather than asserting that
centralization helps.

The clearest paths forward---a non-survivor-biased probe that upgrades the mechanism from calibrated
to predictive, and a second directed-flow substrate that tests the cascade's reach---are set out
above. More broadly, a measured collapse boundary turns ``how should we incentivize a team of
human-shaped learning agents?'' into a question with a number, an error bar, and a named participant
who sets it.

\section*{Ethical Statement}

This work studies when cooperative multi-agent systems fail to coordinate, in a simulated
inventory substrate with no human subjects or personal data. Its intended use is defensive: the
boundary is a safety margin that helps practitioners avoid silent coordination collapse before
deployment, and the minority result flags which participant most needs scrutiny. Two cautions
follow. Raising the centralization dial $w$ trades agent autonomy and local information for
collective reliability, a trade with governance and privacy implications a technical threshold does
not settle; and the behavioral axis we expose could be tuned to manipulate a system's coordination
requirements rather than safeguard them---we report the decomposition and cascade precisely so such
effects stay auditable. Our quantitative claims are scoped to one simulated substrate and should be
re-measured before transfer to real deployments.
