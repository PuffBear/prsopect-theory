\begin{abstract}
Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) can collapse into a degenerate
\emph{lazy-agent} policy when reward centralization is too low, but this failure has only
been described qualitatively. We make it measurable: with a pre-registered collapse
definition and bootstrap confidence intervals, we estimate the critical centralization
$\wcrit$ at which coordination gives way to collapse. Using this boundary as an instrument,
we find that behavioral \emph{loss aversion} ($\lambda>1$) shifts it rightward---loss-averse
agents demand more centralization to coordinate. The direction is monotone in $\lambda$, its
sign is fixed a priori by a mean-field mechanism, and it replicates across four
environment--algorithm settings; the boundary's metastable slide with the training horizon
likewise generalizes. The instrument then exposes two findings: prospect theory's two
components move the boundary in \emph{opposite} directions, and---on a directed-flow
substrate---a single \emph{downstream} loss-averse agent can set the whole system's
requirement, where the average agent would not. We report what generalizes and what is
single-substrate, and frame the directed-flow cascade as a falsifiable prediction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A team of learning agents sharing a task can fall into a trap their designers never
intended: instead of coordinating, every agent quietly defaults to inaction, and the team
settles into a stable, useless equilibrium. Cooperative MARL calls this \emph{coordination
collapse}, with the \emph{lazy-agent} policy as its endpoint~\citep{liu2023lazy,peng2023curo}---an
instance of the sequential social dilemma that arises whenever private incentives drift from
the collective good~\citep{leibo2017multi}. Whether a team falls in is governed by one design
dial, the \emph{reward centralization} $w\in[0,1]$: the share of each agent's reward tied to
the team's outcome rather than its own. Turn $w$ down and an agent's private performance
drowns out its contribution, so cooperating earns no learnable signal and the safe policy
wins. The field knows this only as a direction---\emph{more centralization
helps}~\citep{dmarl2025,liu2023lazy}---and stops there. \emph{Where}, in $w$, a working team
tips into collapse has never been measured. The nearest work mitigates the lazy-agent
pathology without locating a boundary~\citep{liu2023lazy,peng2023curo,weng2026}, or sweeps
neighbouring axes---credit-assignment level~\citep{wang2025maca}, reward
weights~\citep{jiang2018dgn}. Closest in spirit, \citet{yamaguchi2025} build a phase diagram
for independent Q-learning over \emph{environmental} axes (grid size, density), and
\citet{capannoli2026phase} prove a metastable boundary in network opinion dynamics under a
fixed update rule; neither measures a reward-structure threshold in gradient-trained
cooperative policies, with confidence intervals, that a behavioral bias then moves.

Pinning that number down matters now, because the agents being deployed are no longer neutral
optimizers. In automated replenishment, fraud review, and energy dispatch, learning agents
act for or beside people and absorb human preference structure by construction---whether
trained on human choices through preference-aligned objectives that are themselves
prospect-theoretic in form~\citep{ethayarajh2024kto}, or sharing the loop with human
operators~\citep{lalmohammed2025}. The sturdiest such regularity is \emph{loss aversion}: a
loss looms larger than an equal gain~\citep{kahneman1979,tversky1992}. Behavioral economics
knows what this does to one chooser; it is silent on what it does to a \emph{group} learning
to coordinate~\citep{leibo2017multi,lowe2017multi}. A measured collapse boundary is the
instrument that question needs, and it extends a thin existing bridge between behavioral
preferences and collective learning dynamics---inequity aversion in social
dilemmas~\citep{hughes2018inequity}, deterministic limits of multi-agent
learning~\citep{barfuss2019deterministic,hu2019meanfield}---to the prospect-theoretic
preferences that today's training pipelines instill.

We therefore build the ruler first, and treat it as the paper's central object. Following the
posture physics takes toward a phase boundary, we fix a binary collapse criterion in advance,
freeze it, fit $P(\mathrm{collapse}\mid w)$, and bootstrap confidence intervals on $\wcrit$.
(We mean ``phase boundary'' empirically---a sharp, metastable threshold whose
$T\!\to\!\infty$ limit is the boundary proper---not a thermodynamic singularity.) Two design
choices keep the readings honest: we apply cumulative prospect theory (CPT) as a \emph{reward
transform} after blending rather than inside the update, so the optimizer is bit-identical
across conditions and any movement of $\wcrit$ is behavioral; and we re-measure at several
horizons, since a drifting threshold could pass for a constant. Our contributions are: (i) the
first confidence-intervalled phase boundary of coordination collapse in deep cooperative MARL,
with a reusable pre-registered protocol; (ii) the finding that loss aversion shifts it
rightward, a direction whose sign we fix a priori and confirm across four
environment--algorithm settings, with the metastable horizon slide generalizing likewise;
(iii) a decomposition showing prospect theory's two components move it in opposite directions;
and (iv) the result that a downstream loss-averse \emph{minority} can set the boundary on a
directed-flow substrate. Our novelty is narrow: \citet{mengesha2025} show loss aversion moves
a tipping point, but in evolutionary game theory over fixed strategies, and
\citet{lalmohammed2025} bring CPT into cooperative MARL without separating its components or
measuring a boundary. Ours is the measured, CI'd boundary and the component decomposition
against it, in gradient-trained neural policies.

\section{Preliminaries and Method}
\label{sec:method}

In cooperative MARL, $N$ agents share an environment and maximize a joint objective under
CTDE~\citep{lowe2017multi}; the reward-mixing weight $w\in[0,1]$ interpolates purely local
($w{=}0$) and purely global ($w{=}1$) reward. As $w\!\to\!0$ the cooperative signal is diluted
and agents collapse to the safe lazy policy~\citep{liu2023lazy,peng2023curo}. This $w$ is our
sole structural intervention; all else is held fixed, so any boundary shift is attributable to
$w$ and the behavioral parameters. Cumulative prospect theory~\citep{kahneman1979,tversky1992}
gives the value function $v(x)=x^\alpha$ for $x\ge0$ and $-\lambda(-x)^\beta$ for $x<0$, where
$\alpha,\beta\in(0,1]$ govern curvature and $\lambda>1$ is loss aversion
($\alpha{=}\beta{=}1,\lambda{=}1$ is linear utility). We apply it as a reward transform after
blending,
\begin{equation}
  r_i^{\PT}=v\!\left((1-w)\,r_i^{\mathrm{local}}+w\,r^{\mathrm{global}}\right),
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
the decisive difference from \citet{lalmohammed2025}, who fold CPT \emph{into} the
actor--critic update, entangling curvature with loss aversion and confounding any shift with a
change to the optimizer. Transforming only the scalar reward leaves the policy gradient
identical across conditions, so a measured shift is behavioral and the two channels dial
independently. We pre-registered (commit \texttt{03c3fc6}) the binary collapse label
$\mathbf{1}[\bar S<10\wedge\bar\pi\le-127.1]$ ($\bar S$ mean base-stock, $\bar\pi$ mean
profit), robust to $\pm20\%$ threshold perturbation (max $\Delta\wcrit=0.015$; Supplement). For
each condition we fit $P(\mathrm{collapse}\mid w)=\sigma(b_0+b_1 w)$, estimate
$\wcrit=-b_0/b_1$, and bootstrap its 95\% CI; steep high-$\lambda$ cells use Firth penalized
regression, which agrees with the bootstrap throughout. The decomposition compares three
conditions with identical environment and optimizer: \textbf{Null} ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}1,
\lambda{=}1$), \textbf{Curvature} ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}0.88,\lambda{=}1$), and \textbf{Loss
aversion} ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}0.88,\lambda>1$). The primary substrate is
\texttt{NetworkManagement-v1}~\citep{hubbs2020orgym}, a six-node multi-echelon inventory
network; agents run PPO~\citep{schulman2017ppo} (20 seeds/condition, 1{,}524 primary runs). Full
setup and hyperparameters are in the Supplement.

\section{The Boundary and Its Behavioral Shift}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{A sharp, metastable boundary.}
Collapse is a sharp logistic transition in $w$, not a gradual decay: at 300k steps
$\wcrit=0.666$ $[0.635,0.694]$ (transition width $\Delta w=0.199$, $p=3.2\times10^{-7}$), with
collapse fractions rising from near-zero to near-one within $\Delta w<0.3$
(Figure~\ref{fig:boundary}a). The boundary is not a constant: it slides left with the training
horizon, $\wcrit=0.822$ at 150k to $0.666$ at 300k. Intermediate-$w$ collapse is therefore a
\emph{metastable} attractor, not an absorbing state. This slide is not a supply-chain artifact:
under centralized-critic MAPPO it recurs in both transfer environments---on MPE $\wcrit$ falls
$0.82\to0.68$ as the horizon grows $2\mathrm{M}\to8\mathrm{M}$, and on Level-Based Foraging
$0.33\to0.20$ over $1\mathrm{M}\to4\mathrm{M}$, both with non-overlapping intervals
(Figure~\ref{fig:boundary}b). The two supply-chain horizons anchor the $-\ln T$ trend the
mechanism predicts; we did not estimate the asymptote, and additional matched horizons are in
progress.\footnote{Runs at 450k and 900k ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}1$) are queued to convert the
asserted $T\!\to\!\infty$ trend into a fitted one; they are not in this version.} A
basin-control experiment rules out initialization as the driver (Supplement).

\paragraph{Loss aversion raises the boundary; curvature lowers it.}
Against a single canonical null---$\wcrit^{\mathrm{null}}=0.666$ $[0.635,0.694]$, independently
reproduced at $0.685$ by a dedicated experiment (every other null-like number is a labeled
offset, not a separate baseline)---we sweep $\lambda\in\{1,\dots,7\}$ ($\alpha{=}\beta{=}0.88$,
300k). The boundary rises monotonically with loss aversion---$\{0.586,0.715,0.774,0.806,0.839,
0.836,0.851\}$---then saturates near $0.85$ with clear headroom to the $w{=}1$ ceiling, so the
plateau is behavioral, not a ceiling artifact (Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition},
Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}); the endpoints are non-overlapping by $0.221$. Curvature acts in
the \emph{opposite} direction, \emph{lowering} $\wcrit$ to $0.586$ ($-0.100$ $[-0.172,-0.033]$
vs.\ the null). That the two components of a single ``risk-averse'' psychology pull the boundary
in opposite directions is the first of two surprises, and is visible only because there is a
calibrated null to read each component against.

\paragraph{Two ways to count the loss-aversion effect.}
The magnitude depends on the baseline, and we report both explicitly to avoid ambiguity
(Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}). Measured \emph{against the linear null} ($0.685$), the
combined CPT shift is $+0.089$ $[0.041,0.141]$ at $\lambda{=}3$ and $+0.166$ $[0.135,0.202]$ at
$\lambda{=}7$---but this nets loss aversion against curvature's $-0.100$. The \emph{pure
loss-aversion increment}, measured against the curvature baseline ($\lambda{=}1$, $0.586$)---the
correct isolate, since it holds curvature fixed---is larger: $+0.188$ at $\lambda{=}3$ and
$+0.265$ at $\lambda{=}7$. The two are reconciled by the curvature offset ($-0.100+0.265=0.165
\approx0.166$). Throughout, ``the loss-aversion effect'' refers to the increment over the
curvature baseline; the net-vs-null figure is the deployment-relevant total. The full sweep is
tracked by a one-parameter, theory-bounded affine law (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}) to RMSE $0.014$;
high-$\lambda$ cells are steep and rest on a single interior grid point, so a finer
$w\in\{0.80,0.90\}$ grid (queued) would tighten those intervals.

\section{A Mean-Field Mechanism Fixes the Sign}
\label{sec:mechanism}

We give a mechanistic account of why the boundary exists and which way each component moves it.
Following deterministic-limit and mean-field treatments of multi-agent
learning~\citep{barfuss2019deterministic,hu2019meanfield,hussain2025convergence}, we model each
Gaussian-policy PPO agent's mean action as a Langevin variable; the machinery is standard and is
in the Supplement. \emph{We claim the mechanism at the level of sign and functional form, not
magnitude.} Modelling the policy mean as $\mathrm{d}\mu_i=D(w)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\Theta(w)}\,
\mathrm{d}W$, with drift $D$ and a centralization-weighted diffusion $\Theta$ into which partner
exploration enters, the replenishing regime makes an upstream node's inventory a local cost but
a global benefit, giving cooperative drift $D(w)=\eta[wg-(1-w)c]$. Escape from the lazy basin is
governed not by drift but by its ratio to gradient noise,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SNR}(w)=\frac{D(w)}{\sqrt{\Theta(w)}}\gtrsim\kappa_c,
  \label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}
and the crossing defines $\wcrit$. Because escape is a Kramers first-passage problem
($\tau\sim e^{\Delta U/\Theta}$), collapse switches abruptly across $\wcrit$ (the sharp fits)
and $\wcrit(T)$ falls as $T$ grows (the metastable slide)---both predicted without fitting. We do
\emph{not} derive the numerical value of $\wcrit$: $c,g,\sigma,\kappa_c$ are calibrated, and our
pre-registered direct drift probe was \emph{inconclusive}---it reproduces the matched-horizon
boundary in collapse fraction but does not localize $\wcrit$, because survivor bias in the
contested band confounds the conditioned drift (Supplement). The magnitude therefore remains
calibrated; the mechanism's predictive content is the sign and the form.

The sign is a theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Sign invariance of the loss-aversion shift]\label{thm:sign}
Let the order parameter $x$ evolve as $\mathrm{d}x_t=F(x_t,w,\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}t+
\sqrt{\Sigma(x_t,N)}\,\mathrm{d}W_t$ with $\Sigma>0$. If $\partial_\lambda F<0$ on the escape
interval---i.e.\ loss aversion scales only the loss domain and so inflates gradient noise---then
$\partial_\lambda \wcrit^{\mathrm{Emp}}>0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The Kramers barrier $\Delta U=\int 2F/\Sigma\,\mathrm{d}z$ has $\partial_\lambda\Delta U<0$
pointwise, since $\Sigma>0$ and $\partial_\lambda F<0$ throughout, independent of the sign of
$F$; a lower barrier shortens escape ($\partial_\lambda\tau<0$) while $\partial_w F>0$ lengthens
it. The implicit function theorem on $\tau(w,\lambda)=T$ gives $\mathrm{d}\wcrit^{\mathrm{Emp}}/
\mathrm{d}\lambda=-\partial_\lambda\tau/\partial_w\tau>0$. The magnitude depends on the
intractable $\Sigma$; the direction does not. The same argument with curvature's drift signs
reversed yields the opposite shift.
\end{proof}

We are candid that the theorem \emph{encodes} the sign of $\partial_\lambda F$ as a premise
rather than deriving it from first principles; its content is that this local sign propagates to
the global boundary regardless of the intractable noise---which is exactly what makes the
direction (but not the magnitude) predictable. The same drift split fixes the functional form:
only the loss domain scales with $\lambda$, giving $\wcrit(\lambda)=\phi(\lambda)/(\phi(\lambda)
+g/c)$ with $\phi(\lambda)=1+\rho(\lambda-1)$ and $\rho\in[0,1]$ bounded by the theory. Fixing
$g/c$ and $\rho=0.61$ from a subset and testing out of sample, the one-parameter law tracks the
full seven-point sweep to RMSE $0.014$. This law is \emph{single-substrate}: it does not transfer
to LBF (\S\ref{sec:generalization}), a limit we report rather than hide.

\section{The Direction Generalizes; the Magnitudes Do Not}
\label{sec:generalization}

The mechanism commits in advance to a direction: loss aversion inflates gradient noise and so
raises $\wcrit$. If that is right rather than an artifact of one substrate or optimizer, the sign
should survive changing both. We test on two further environments and a second algorithm,
matching the learner to each environment's learnability (independent PPO where coordination is
locally learnable; centralized-critic MAPPO~\citep{yu2022surprising} where the reward is
shared-global). The direction is robust in every setting; the absolute level, the curvature sign,
and the functional form are not (Figure~\ref{fig:generalization}, Table~\ref{tab:generalization}).
On the \emph{supply chain under MAPPO} (only the learner changes), $\wcrit$ rises $0.796\to0.889$
over $\lambda{=}1\to7$, monotone from $\lambda{=}2$, endpoints non-overlapping; the level differs
at low $\lambda$ but converges with PPO by $\lambda\!\geq\!4$. On \emph{MPE} the loss-aversion
excess is $+0.158$ $[0.108,0.212]$ at $\lambda{=}7$, but the curvature effect is null/opposite
($+0.042$, CI spanning zero), and on \emph{LBF} curvature moves the boundary \emph{right}---both
confirming the curvature sign is substrate-specific. The supply chain's affine law misfits LBF by
RMSE $0.15$. Thus the \emph{method}---a CI'd boundary that loss aversion shifts---generalizes
across three environments and two algorithms, while the affine constants and the curvature sign do
not.

Two ablations confirm the mechanism's scope conditions. Replacing the continuous policy gradient
with value-based learners (QMIX, IQL) on a discretized action space erases the boundary---but so
does discretizing PPO alone, isolating coarse discretization (not the value-based paradigm) as the
cause, as the continuous-gradient mechanism predicts. And on shared-reward tasks the boundary is
measurable only with a centralized critic: independent PPO collapses at \emph{every} $w$ on MPE (a
credit-assignment floor), while MAPPO recovers coordination. Both are detailed in the Supplement;
each is a confirmed boundary condition, not a negative result.

\section{A Minority Sets the Boundary}
\label{sec:minority}

The decomposition treats the population as homogeneous, but deployed systems mix loss-averse and
risk-neutral participants. Assigning $\lambda{=}7$ to selected nodes and $\lambda{=}1$ to the rest
(substrate, PPO config, label, grid identical to the main sweep), we find the boundary is set by a
\emph{minority}, and \emph{network position} is decisive (Table~\ref{tab:minority}). A single
\emph{downstream} loss-averse node drives $\wcrit=0.853$ $[0.849,0.858]$---statistically
indistinguishable from a \emph{fully} loss-averse population ($0.851$): one biased node downstream
imposes the centralization requirement of the entire network, and two downstream nodes add nothing
further. The same node \emph{upstream} shifts the boundary far less, to $0.726$ $[0.689,0.763]$.
Loss aversion thus cascades \emph{upstream} along the ordering chain but not downstream, a
directional asymmetry mirroring the substrate's bullwhip structure. That a behavioral minority can
move a tipping point is established in evolutionary game theory~\citep{mengesha2025}; ours is the
deep-MARL instantiation, where the effect emerges from gradient-learning dynamics and carries a
concrete network-position signature. The operational reading is sharp: in a mixed human--agent
chain, the required centralization is set by the most downstream loss-averse participant, not the
mean.

We are explicit about the limits of this result, which is the thinnest part of the paper. It uses
binary $\lambda\in\{1,7\}$ on one topology, and tests four configurations (one upstream, one
downstream, two downstream, all); a continuous-$\lambda$ sweep and intermediate node positions are
in progress to characterize the cascade as a function of position and bias strength.\footnote{Runs
varying node index along the chain and sweeping $\lambda$ continuously at a fixed downstream node
are queued; this version reports the four binary configurations.} The result is also single-substrate
\emph{by construction}: ``which agent sets the boundary'' is well-posed only where a directed
resource flow defines upstream/downstream. MPE (spatial) and LBF (foraging) have none, so the
question does not type-check there; a search for a second directed-flow cooperative-MARL substrate
found none reachable (Supplement). We therefore establish the effect where its precondition holds
and frame its reach as a \emph{falsifiable, structurally specified} prediction: in any cooperative
domain with a directed resource flow and a learning-reachable boundary, a downstream loss-averse
minority should set it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

That probability weighting and loss aversion exert opposite effects on the boundary is, to our
knowledge, novel; prior work applies prospect theory as a unified model without decomposing
it~\citep{lalmohammed2025,ghaemi2024risk}. The operational reading is concrete: a system of
$\lambda{=}7$ agents needs $w>0.851$ for reliable coordination against $w>0.685$ for risk-neutral
agents---a $+0.166$ deployment margin that scales with $\lambda$, so domains nearer
$\lambda\!\approx\!2$--$3$ incur a smaller but positive cost. Where centralization is capped by
privacy or communication cost, the boundary reframes the problem, exposing $\lambda$---the agents'
effective loss aversion, shaped by the training objective---as a second lever. Our phase-diagram
framing sits within a recognized line of multiagent phase-transition
results~\citep{yamaguchi2025,capannoli2026phase} and the broader project of cooperative
AI~\citep{dafoe2020open,hammond2025multiagent,barfuss2020caring}; its distinctive contribution is
a boundary set by the policy-gradient signal-to-noise ratio rather than a fixed update rule.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
We state these plainly, as several bound the contribution. (i)~The \emph{quantitative} core---the
affine $\lambda$-law, the metastable magnitudes, the cascade---is single-substrate; the affine law
explicitly fails to transfer, and the cascade is one topology with binary $\lambda$. We replicate
the boundary, the loss-aversion direction, and the metastable slide across environments and a
second algorithm, but the precise numbers are a property of one simulator and one compute budget.
(ii)~The mechanism is calibrated, not predictive: it fixes sign and form, the direct drift probe was
inconclusive, and the sign-invariance theorem encodes its key premise rather than deriving it.
(iii)~$\lambda{=}7$ is an upper, high-stakes bound rather than a typical human value
($\lambda\!\approx\!2.25$); the deployment-relevant margins are at $\lambda\!\approx\!3$. (iv)~The
high-$\lambda$ grid is coarse, and the horizon trend rests on two clean points; finer-grid and
additional-horizon runs are in progress. None of these undercut the central, replicated claim---loss
aversion raises the coordination requirement---but they scope what is measured versus what is
predicted.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented the first confidence-intervalled phase diagram of cooperative-MARL coordination
collapse, characterizing $\wcrit$ as a function of reward structure, training horizon, and
prospect-theoretic loss aversion. Loss aversion impedes coordination---raising the requirement, a
direction we predict a priori and confirm across four environment--algorithm settings, with the
metastable slide generalizing likewise. Two further results sharpen the picture: prospect theory's
two components act in opposite directions, and a downstream loss-averse minority can set the
